# Before the Big Bang: plausible hypotheses

Notes from a conversation on 2026-10-22. This is speculation, not a result: none
of it is settled, and the credences are personal guesses.

## 1. What is actually known

"The Big Bang" means two different things:

1. **The hot, dense early universe.** About 13.8 billion years ago the universe
   was hot, dense and expanding. The evidence is overwhelming: the cosmic
   microwave background (CMB), the measured amounts of light elements, and the
   expansion of space.
2. **The "t = 0" singularity.** This is not an observation. It comes from running
   general relativity backwards past the point where it stops being valid. A
   singularity usually means the theory has broken down, not that the universe
   began there.

So "before the Big Bang" really asks what replaces general relativity at those
densities. That needs quantum gravity, which we don't have.

## 2. The first step back is probably inflation

Before the hot phase, a field (the "inflaton") drove a very rapid, accelerating
expansion. When it decayed, its energy became hot matter and radiation. On this
view, **what did "the bang" was the end of inflation**.

Inflation correctly predicted several things measured later:

- Space is flat.
- The CMB has the same temperature in regions that could never have been in contact.
- The seed fluctuations are almost scale-invariant: the spectral index is about
  0.965, close to 1 but not equal to it.

That moves the question back one step: what came before inflation, and what
started it?

## 3. Hypotheses for what came before

| Hypothesis | What was "before" | What started it | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **No-boundary** (Hartle–Hawking 1983) | Nothing. Near the start, time turns into a fourth direction of space. Asking what came before is like asking what is south of the South Pole. | Nothing "caused" it. The universe is a self-contained quantum state. | Elegant. Whether its calculation is mathematically stable is disputed (Feldbrugge–Lehners–Turok). |
| **Tunnelling from nothing** (Vilenkin 1982) | No space, no time, no matter. | A quantum tunnelling event creates a tiny closed universe that then inflates. | Similar to no-boundary. Needs the laws of quantum mechanics to already exist. |
| **Eternal inflation / multiverse** | A forever-inflating "sea"; our universe is one bubble where inflation ended. | Inflation never stops everywhere at once; bubbles keep forming. | Natural in many inflation models. The Borde–Guth–Vilenkin theorem says it still can't be infinitely old, so it pushes the beginning back without removing it. |
| **Big Bounce** (loop quantum cosmology, ekpyrotic models) | An earlier, contracting universe. | Quantum-gravity effects make space resist being crushed; it rebounds at about the Planck density. | Actively studied. Some versions need no inflation at all. |
| **Conformal cyclic cosmology** (Penrose) | The infinite future of an earlier "aeon". | When only massless radiation is left, size and time lose meaning; a dead, infinitely big universe is mathematically the same as a new, tiny, hot one. | Claimed CMB signals ("Hawking points") are widely disputed. |
| **Born inside a black hole** (Smolin, Popławski) | The inside of a black hole in a parent universe. | The collapse rebounds into a new expanding region. | Predicts our constants favour making black holes, which is partly testable. |
| **CPT-mirror universe** (Boyle–Finn–Turok 2018) | A mirror "anti-universe" running backwards in time on the other side of t = 0. | The two halves form one symmetric whole; there was no event to cause. | Very testable: predicts no primordial gravitational waves and a massless lightest neutrino. |

## 4. My bet

- **About 70%: the hot Big Bang was the end of inflation.** Recent Atacama
  Cosmology Telescope (ACT) data put mild pressure on the simplest inflation
  models, not on inflation itself.
- **Before inflation, the lead hypothesis is that time itself emerges.** In quantum
  gravity, the equations for the universe as a whole (the Wheeler–DeWitt equation)
  contain no time variable. Time appears only inside the universe, as clocks
  become correlated with everything else. Then "before" and "cause" stop working
  near the beginning, like "north" at the North Pole. Causes need time, and there
  wasn't any yet.
- **Second place: a bounce.** Conservative: the universe keeps existing and quantum
  gravity just prevents infinite density. It gives a real "before".
- **Every option hits the same wall.** Even "from nothing" assumes the laws of
  quantum mechanics already exist. Physics explains how things evolve under laws;
  it cannot explain why there are laws at all.

## 5. A testable version

Inflation erases what came before it by stretching it enormously, so the only
possible traces sit at the very largest scales we can see. If inflation lasted
only slightly longer than its minimum (about 60 e-folds), the pre-inflation era
could leave fingerprints in the largest-scale features of the CMB, which does have
known oddities there:

- unexpectedly weak temperature variation across the largest angles;
- a slight difference between the two halves of the sky;
- the "Cold Spot".

**Hypothesis:** some of these are faint evidence of what came before inflation,
not chance. Tests:

1. **Polarisation maps** (LiteBIRD, Simons Observatory): an independent second
   look at the largest scales. Same oddities → probably real physics.
2. **Primordial gravitational waves:** a detection strongly supports inflation and
   rules out the CPT-mirror universe and the ekpyrotic bounce.
3. **Neutrino masses:** if the lightest neutrino has mass, the CPT-mirror universe
   is dead.

## 6. How can a human imagine "no time"?

We can't picture it directly: imagining is itself something that happens in time.
But we also can't picture four-dimensional space or curved space, and we still
understand them through analogies and maths. Some analogies that help:

- **Time is like temperature.** Temperature is real, but it only exists for many
  particles together; asking for the temperature of one atom is ill-posed. Time
  may be the same kind of thing: a collective property that works well almost
  everywhere and fades near the beginning. "What happened before time?" may be
  ill-posed in the same way.
- **The film reel.** A film shows motion, but the reel just sits on the shelf,
  with every frame present at once. "Time" in the film is just the order of the
  frames. The universe may be like the reel, not like the projection.
- **The photograph with a clock** (Page–Wootters, 1983). Take one still photo of
  a whole universe containing a clock and a cup of coffee. Nothing in it moves.
  Yet inside the photo, "clock says 3" goes with "coffee hot" and "clock says 4"
  goes with "coffee cold". Someone inside would experience change; someone
  outside sees one frozen state. This was demonstrated in a toy experiment with
  entangled photons (Moreva et al., 2014).
- **The South Pole.** Walk south and eventually "further south" stops meaning
  anything. There is no wall and no edge, the direction just runs out. In the
  no-boundary picture, time runs out the same way.

The mental shift: replace "first there was nothing, then something" with "the
universe is a structure, and time is one feature inside it that works well almost
everywhere and fades near one edge".

## 7. If there is no time, is there no space either?

Not necessarily. Relativity weaves space and time into one spacetime, so it feels
like they should stand or fall together. But in the main quantum-gravity pictures
they behave differently:

- **Space without time (Wheeler–DeWitt).** The wave function of the universe is a
  function of possible *shapes of 3D space*, with no time variable at all. Space
  is all there is, and time is read off from space itself (for example, the size
  of the universe serves as the clock). Barbour's "timeless" physics is the same
  idea: reality is the collection of all possible shapes ("Nows").
- **Time turns into space (Hartle–Hawking).** Near the beginning, time becomes a
  fourth direction of space. So at the start there are four dimensions of space
  and none of time: space without time again.
- **Both emerge from something else.**
  - *Entanglement builds space* (Van Raamsdonk 2010, holography): in some models,
    reducing quantum entanglement between two regions literally pulls space apart.
    Distance would be a measure of how entangled things are.
  - *Causal sets* (Sorkin): reality is a discrete set of events with only an order
    ("this can influence that"). "Order + number = geometry": counting events gives
    volume, and the order gives the causal structure. Space and time both emerge.
  - *Loop quantum gravity*: space is a network of tiny chunks of area and volume.
    There is no background space; the network *is* space.

Distance itself may also lose its meaning at the Planck length (about 1.6×10⁻³⁵ m),
where geometry is expected to fluctuate wildly ("spacetime foam"). Below that,
"how far apart?" may be as ill-posed as "what time is it?" at the beginning.

**Short answer:** most likely both time and space are emergent, but they need not
fade together. Several leading pictures keep something space-like after time is
gone, and in others both come from something that is neither: relations, not
distances.

## 8. What the data already say: n_s and r

Two numbers measured from the CMB summarise the seed fluctuations:

- **n_s, the spectral index.** It says how the strength of the fluctuations changes
  with size. n_s = 1 means "the same at every scale"; slightly below 1 means large
  scales are a bit stronger.
- **r, the tensor-to-scalar ratio.** It is the strength of primordial gravitational
  waves compared with the density ripples. Inflation makes gravitational waves
  whose size depends on how much energy drove it. Most bounce models make
  essentially none.

Each inflation model is a potential V(φ), and it predicts a point (n_s, r) once you
fix N, the number of e-folds between when today's large scales were created and the
end of inflation. N is between about 50 and 60; the exact value depends on how the
universe reheated. `ns_r.py` computes these points with the standard slow-roll
formulas, and `ns_r.png` plots them against the data.

**Current limits** (68% for n_s, 95% for r):

| Data | n_s | r |
|---|---|---|
| Planck 2018 (Planck Collaboration VI, 2020) | 0.9649 ± 0.0042 | |
| BICEP/Keck 2018 (BICEP/Keck Collaboration, PRL 2021) | | < 0.036 |
| ACT DR6 + Planck + lensing + DESI DR1 BAO (Louis et al. 2025) | 0.974 ± 0.003 | |
| All CMB: Planck + SPT-3G + ACT + BICEP/Keck (Balkenhol et al. 2026) | 0.9682 ± 0.0032 | < 0.034 |
| Same + DESI DR2 BAO (Balkenhol et al. 2026) | 0.9728 ± 0.0029 | < 0.034 |

The two last rows disagree a little. Adding the DESI galaxy-distance data (BAO)
pushes n_s up. The authors call the CMB–DESI difference marginal and so far
unexplained (Balkenhol et al. 2026; McDonough–Ferreira 2025 get the same
upward shift for every CMB data set).

**Results** (N = 50 → 60; σ = distance from the measured n_s):

| Model | n_s | r | vs CMB only | vs CMB + DESI |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| φ⁴ (chaotic) | 0.941 → 0.951 | 0.26–0.31 | dead (r and n_s) | dead |
| φ² (chaotic, the textbook model) | 0.960 → 0.967 | 0.13–0.16 | dead (r) | dead |
| φ, φ^(2/3) (axion monodromy) | 0.970 → 0.978 | 0.04–0.08 | dead (r) | dead (r), although n_s fits |
| Natural inflation, f = 7 | 0.957 → 0.963 | 0.07–0.09 | dead (r) | dead |
| Starobinsky R² / Higgs | 0.962 → 0.968 | ≈ 0.003–0.004 | fine (−2.1σ → 0σ) | strained (−3.9σ → −1.7σ) |
| E-model α-attractor, α = 10 | 0.964 → 0.970 | ≈ 0.02 | fine | strained at small N |
| T-model α-attractor, α = 1/3 | 0.960 → 0.967 | ≈ 0.001 | fine | strained |
| Polynomial attractor k = 2 (Kallosh–Linde 2022) | 0.969 → 0.974 | ≈ 0.003–0.004 | fine | fine |
| Polynomial attractor k = 4 | 0.966 → 0.972 | < 0.001 | fine | fine |

What the data already rule in or out:

1. **The simplest "big field rolling down a parabola" inflation is dead.** That kill
   comes from r, the missing gravitational waves, not from n_s. Surviving inflation has
   a flat plateau: a potential that is almost constant for large φ.
2. **Starobinsky / Higgs inflation fits the CMB alone but is under pressure once DESI
   is added**, at about 2–4σ depending on N. This is the "mild pressure" mentioned
   in section 4. Whether it is real depends on the unexplained CMB–DESI difference.
   Balkenhol et al. 2026 and McDonough–Ferreira 2025 both say that plateaus
   approached polynomially rather than exponentially (polynomial α-attractors) fit
   either way.
3. **Inflation itself is not in trouble.** Plateau models cover the whole allowed n_s
   range with r from 0.02 down to below 0.001.

What this means for the "before" stories of section 3:

- **No-boundary, tunnelling, eternal inflation.** They describe how inflation
  *started*, not which potential it ran on, so they inherit whatever plateau model is
  right. The data say nothing against them. They also say nothing *for* them beyond
  "inflation is fine".
- **Bounce followed by inflation** (loop quantum cosmology). Its n_s and r are the
  same as inflation's. The bounce could only show up at the very largest scales, as
  slightly *less* power there (Ashtekar–Gupt 2016; Ashtekar et al., PRL 2020). That links it to the anomalies
  of section 5.
- **Bounce instead of inflation.**
  - *Ekpyrotic/cyclic:* r is essentially zero, because the slow contraction does not
    amplify gravitational waves (Ijjas–Lehners–Steinhardt 2014). n_s depends on the
    model and can be tuned to the measured value. The data allow it. The test that
    could kill it is a detection of r.
  - *Matter bounce:* the simplest version predicts n_s = 1 exactly, which is about
    10σ away from 0.968, and r too large. Variants such as the ΛCDM bounce
    (Cai–Wilson-Ewing 2015) get a slight red tilt and small r, at the price of extra
    assumptions.
- **CPT-mirror universe.** It predicts r = 0 (Boyle–Finn–Turok 2018). Turok–Boyle
  2023 (a preprint) claim to derive the amplitude and tilt from Standard Model
  couplings with no free parameter. The abstract gives no number, so I haven't
  plotted it. The data allow it so far.
- **Conformal cyclic cosmology, black-hole origin.** Neither gives a definite (n_s, r)
  prediction, so this test doesn't touch them.

**The coming decisive measurement is r.** Simons Observatory aims at σ(r) = 0.003
(SO Collaboration 2019) and the LiteBIRD satellite at δr ≈ 0.001 (LiteBIRD
Collaboration 2023). Only LiteBIRD-level precision separates the two outcomes:

- **r ≈ 0.003 detected** (the Starobinsky / k = 2 value): strong evidence for plateau
  inflation, and the end of the ekpyrotic bounce and the CPT-mirror universe.
- **Nothing above ~0.002:** Starobinsky and Higgs inflation die. Inflation survives
  only in models with tiny r (like k = 4 above). The no-inflation bounce and the
  CPT-mirror universe gain ground.

Either way, the bet in section 4 gets tested within about ten years.
