# Before the Big Bang: plausible hypotheses

Notes from a conversation on 2026-10-22. This is speculation, not a result: none
of it is settled, and the credences are personal guesses.

## 1. What is actually known

"The Big Bang" means two different things:

1. **The hot, dense early universe.** About 13.8 billion years ago the universe
   was hot, dense and expanding. The evidence is overwhelming: the cosmic
   microwave background (CMB), the measured amounts of light elements, and the
   expansion of space.
2. **The "t = 0" singularity.** This is not an observation. It comes from running
   general relativity backwards past the point where it stops being valid. A
   singularity usually means the theory has broken down, not that the universe
   began there.

So "before the Big Bang" really asks what replaces general relativity at those
densities. That needs quantum gravity, which we don't have.

## 2. The first step back is probably inflation

Before the hot phase, a field (the "inflaton") drove a very rapid, accelerating
expansion. When it decayed, its energy became hot matter and radiation. On this
view, **what did "the bang" was the end of inflation**.

Inflation explains two puzzles it was designed to solve (Guth 1981), and it made one
real prediction that was confirmed later:

- Space is flat. (A motivation, later confirmed precisely.)
- The CMB has the same temperature in regions that could never have been in contact.
  (Known since 1965; this "horizon problem" is what inflation was invented for.)
- **The prediction:** the seed fluctuations are almost, but not exactly, the same
  strength at every scale (Mukhanov–Chibisov 1981). The spectral index n_s is
  0.968 ± 0.003 (all CMB data, Balkenhol et al. 2026), close to 1 but not equal to it.

That moves the question back one step: what came before inflation, and what
started it?

## 3. Hypotheses for what came before

| Hypothesis | What was "before" | What started it | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **No-boundary** (Hartle–Hawking 1983) | Nothing. Near the start, time turns into a fourth direction of space. Asking what came before is like asking what is south of the South Pole. | Nothing "caused" it. The universe is a self-contained quantum state. | Elegant. Whether its calculation is mathematically stable is disputed (Feldbrugge–Lehners–Turok). |
| **Tunnelling from nothing** (Vilenkin 1982) | No space, no time, no matter. | A quantum tunnelling event creates a tiny closed universe that then inflates. | Similar to no-boundary. Needs the laws of quantum mechanics to already exist. |
| **Eternal inflation / multiverse** | A forever-inflating "sea"; our universe is one bubble where inflation ended. | Inflation never stops everywhere at once; bubbles keep forming. | Natural in many inflation models. The Borde–Guth–Vilenkin theorem (2003) says inflation alone can't extend infinitely into the past: something else, not necessarily a beginning, must come before it. |
| **Big Bounce** (loop quantum cosmology, ekpyrotic models) | An earlier, contracting universe. | In loop quantum cosmology, quantum-gravity effects make space resist being crushed, and it rebounds at about 0.4 of the Planck density. Ekpyrotic models use a slow contraction instead; their bounce is either left unspecified or entirely classical, well below the Planck density (Ijjas–Steinhardt 2016). | Actively studied. Some versions need no inflation at all. |
| **Conformal cyclic cosmology** (Penrose) | The infinite future of an earlier "aeon". | When only massless radiation is left, size and time lose meaning; a dead, infinitely big universe is mathematically the same as a new, tiny, hot one. | Claimed CMB signals ("Hawking points") are widely disputed. |
| **Born inside a black hole** (Smolin, Popławski) | The inside of a black hole in a parent universe. | The collapse rebounds into a new expanding region. | Smolin's version predicts our constants favour making black holes. His concrete test was a maximum neutron-star mass (a star above about 2.5 solar masses would refute it, Smolin 1997). Stars of about 2 solar masses are now observed, which some argue already puts it under pressure. |
| **CPT-mirror universe** (Boyle–Finn–Turok 2018) | A mirror "anti-universe" running backwards in time on the other side of t = 0. | The two halves form one symmetric whole; there was no event to cause. | Very testable: predicts no primordial gravitational waves and a massless lightest neutrino. |

## 4. My bet

- **About 70%: the hot Big Bang was the end of inflation.** Recent CMB and galaxy
  data (ACT, SPT, DESI) put pressure on the favourite inflation models, up to 3.9σ
  for Starobinsky inflation once DESI is included (section 8), but not on inflation
  itself.
- **Before inflation, the lead hypothesis is that time itself emerges.** In quantum
  gravity, the equations for the universe as a whole (the Wheeler–DeWitt equation)
  contain no time variable. Time appears only inside the universe, as clocks
  become correlated with everything else. Then "before" and "cause" stop working
  near the beginning, like "north" at the North Pole. Causes need time, and there
  wasn't any yet.
- **Second place: a bounce.** Conservative: the universe keeps existing and quantum
  gravity just prevents infinite density. It gives a real "before".
- **Every option hits the same wall.** Even "from nothing" assumes the laws of
  quantum mechanics already exist. Physics explains how things evolve under laws;
  it cannot explain why there are laws at all.

## 5. A testable version

Inflation erases what came before it by stretching it enormously, so the only
possible traces sit at the very largest scales we can see. If inflation lasted
only slightly longer than its minimum (about 60 e-folds), the pre-inflation era
could leave fingerprints in the largest-scale features of the CMB, which does have
known oddities there:

- unexpectedly weak temperature variation across the largest angles;
- a slight difference between the two halves of the sky;
- the "Cold Spot".

**Hypothesis:** some of these are faint evidence of what came before inflation,
not chance. Tests:

1. **Polarisation maps** at the largest scales (mainly the LiteBIRD satellite; ground
   telescopes like the Simons Observatory cover somewhat smaller scales): an
   independent second look. Same oddities → probably real physics.
2. **Primordial gravitational waves:** a detection strongly supports inflation and
   rules out the CPT-mirror universe and the ekpyrotic bounce.
3. **Neutrino masses** (section 10): if the lightest neutrino has mass, the CPT-mirror universe
   is dead.

## 6. How can a human imagine "no time"?

We can't picture it directly: imagining is itself something that happens in time.
But we also can't picture four-dimensional space or curved space, and we still
understand them through analogies and maths. Some analogies that help:

- **Time is like temperature.** Temperature is real, but it only exists for many
  particles together; asking for the temperature of one atom is ill-posed. Time
  may be the same kind of thing: a collective property that works well almost
  everywhere and fades near the beginning. "What happened before time?" may be
  ill-posed in the same way.
- **The film reel.** A film shows motion, but the reel just sits on the shelf,
  with every frame present at once. "Time" in the film is just the order of the
  frames. The universe may be like the reel, not like the projection.
- **The photograph with a clock** (Page–Wootters, 1983). Take one still photo of
  a whole universe containing a clock and a cup of coffee. Nothing in it moves.
  Yet inside the photo, "clock says 3" goes with "coffee hot" and "clock says 4"
  goes with "coffee cold". Someone inside would experience change; someone
  outside sees one frozen state. This was demonstrated in a toy experiment with
  entangled photons (Moreva et al., 2014).
- **The South Pole.** Walk south and eventually "further south" stops meaning
  anything. There is no wall and no edge, the direction just runs out. In the
  no-boundary picture, time runs out the same way.

The mental shift: replace "first there was nothing, then something" with "the
universe is a structure, and time is one feature inside it that works well almost
everywhere and fades near one edge".

## 7. If there is no time, is there no space either?

Not necessarily. Relativity weaves space and time into one spacetime, so it feels
like they should stand or fall together. But in the main quantum-gravity pictures
they behave differently:

- **Space without time (Wheeler–DeWitt).** The wave function of the universe is a
  function of possible *shapes of 3D space*, with no time variable at all. Space
  is all there is, and time is read off from space itself (for example, the size
  of the universe serves as the clock). Barbour's "timeless" physics is the same
  idea: reality is the collection of all possible shapes ("Nows").
- **Time turns into space (Hartle–Hawking).** Near the beginning, time becomes a
  fourth direction of space. So at the start there are four dimensions of space
  and none of time: space without time again.
- **Both emerge from something else.**
  - *Entanglement builds space* (Van Raamsdonk 2010, holography): in some models,
    reducing quantum entanglement between two regions literally pulls space apart.
    Distance would be a measure of how entangled things are.
  - *Causal sets* (Sorkin): reality is a discrete set of events with only an order
    ("this can influence that"). "Order + number = geometry": counting events gives
    volume, and the order gives the causal structure. Space and time both emerge.
  - *Loop quantum gravity*: space is a network of tiny chunks of area and volume.
    There is no background space; the network *is* space.

Distance itself may also lose its meaning at the Planck length (about 1.6×10⁻³⁵ m),
where geometry is expected to fluctuate wildly ("spacetime foam"). Below that,
"how far apart?" may be as ill-posed as "what time is it?" at the beginning.

**Short answer:** most likely both time and space are emergent, but they need not
fade together. Several leading pictures keep something space-like after time is
gone, and in others both come from something that is neither: relations, not
distances.

## 8. What the data already say: n_s and r

Two numbers measured from the CMB summarise the seed fluctuations:

- **n_s, the spectral index.** It says how the strength of the fluctuations changes
  with size. n_s = 1 means "the same at every scale"; slightly below 1 means large
  scales are a bit stronger.
- **r, the tensor-to-scalar ratio.** It is the strength of primordial gravitational
  waves compared with the density ripples. Inflation makes gravitational waves
  whose size depends on how much energy drove it. Ekpyrotic bounces and the CPT
  mirror make essentially none. (A bounce *followed by* inflation makes inflation's.)

Each inflation model is a potential V(φ), and it predicts a point (n_s, r) once you
fix N, the number of e-folds between when today's large scales were created and the
end of inflation. N is between about 50 and 60; the exact value depends on how the
universe reheated. `ns_r.py` computes these points with the standard slow-roll
formulas, and `ns_r.png` plots them against the data.

**Current limits** (68% for n_s, 95% for r):

| Data | n_s | r |
|---|---|---|
| Planck 2018 (Planck Collaboration VI, 2020) | 0.9649 ± 0.0042 | |
| BICEP/Keck 2018 (BICEP/Keck Collaboration, PRL 2021) | | < 0.036 |
| ACT DR6 + Planck + lensing + DESI DR1 BAO (Louis et al. 2025) | 0.974 ± 0.003 | |
| All CMB: Planck + SPT-3G + ACT + BICEP/Keck (Balkenhol et al. 2026) | 0.9682 ± 0.0032 | < 0.034 |
| Same + DESI DR2 BAO (Balkenhol et al. 2026) | 0.9728 ± 0.0029 | < 0.034 |

The two last rows disagree a little. Adding the DESI galaxy-distance data (BAO)
pushes n_s up. The authors call the CMB–DESI difference marginal and so far
unexplained (Balkenhol et al. 2026; McDonough–Ferreira 2025 get the same
upward shift for every CMB data set).

**Results** (N = 50 → 60; σ = distance from the measured n_s). One rule for every row:
"fits" if every N from 50 to 60 is within 2σ, "fits for some N" if only part of the
range is, "disfavoured" if none is, "dead" if r is above the limit.

N is not free for every model: the reheating physics fixes it near 51 for Starobinsky
and 55 for Higgs inflation (Balkenhol et al. 2026). They find Starobinsky 2.0σ and Higgs
1.3σ from the CMB alone, and Starobinsky 3.9σ and Higgs 2.9σ once DESI is added.

What the data already rule in or out:

1. **The simplest "big field rolling down a parabola" inflation is dead.** That kill
   comes from r, the missing gravitational waves, not from n_s. The survivors are
   plateau models (a potential that is almost constant for large φ) and very shallow
   monomials like φ^(1/3), whose r ≈ 0.02–0.03 sits just under the limit. Balkenhol
   et al. 2026 find that, with DESI and r both taken into account, "a range of
   monomial inflaton potentials" is compatible at < 2σ.
2. **Starobinsky / Higgs inflation fits the CMB alone but is under real pressure once
   DESI is added**: 3.9σ for Starobinsky and 2.9σ for Higgs at their reheating-fixed N.
   This is the pressure mentioned in section 4. Whether it is real depends on the
   unexplained CMB–DESI difference. Balkenhol et al. 2026 show that plateaus approached
   polynomially rather than exponentially (polynomial α-attractors) fit either way.
   McDonough–Ferreira 2025 also find that adding DESI favours them.
3. **Inflation itself is not in trouble.** Plateau models cover the whole allowed n_s
   range with r from 0.02 down to below 0.001.

What this means for the "before" stories of section 3:

- **No-boundary, tunnelling, eternal inflation.** They describe how inflation
  *started*, not which potential it ran on, so they inherit whatever plateau model is
  right. The data say nothing against them. They also say nothing *for* them beyond
  "inflation is fine".
- **Bounce followed by inflation** (loop quantum cosmology). Its n_s and r are the
  same as inflation's. The bounce could only show up at the very largest scales, as
  slightly *less* power there (Ashtekar–Gupt 2016; Ashtekar et al., PRL 2020). That links it to the anomalies
  of section 5.
- **Bounce instead of inflation.**
  - *Ekpyrotic/cyclic:* r is essentially zero, because the slow contraction does not
    amplify gravitational waves (Boyle–Steinhardt–Turok 2004). n_s depends on the
    model and can be tuned to the measured value. The data allow it. The test that
    could kill it is a detection of r.
  - *Matter bounce:* the simplest version predicts n_s = 1 exactly, which is about
    10σ away from 0.968, and r too large. Variants such as the ΛCDM bounce
    (Cai–Wilson-Ewing 2015) get a slight red tilt and small r, at the price of extra
    assumptions.
- **CPT-mirror universe.** It predicts r = 0 (Boyle–Finn–Turok 2018). Turok–Boyle
  2023 (a preprint, arXiv:2302.00344) claim to derive the amplitude and tilt from
  Standard Model couplings with no free parameter, "subject to two key theoretical
  assumptions". The abstract gives no number, so I haven't
  plotted it. The data allow it so far.
- **Conformal cyclic cosmology, black-hole origin.** Neither gives a definite (n_s, r)
  prediction, so this test doesn't touch them.

**The coming decisive measurement is r.** Simons Observatory aims at σ(r) = 0.003
(SO Collaboration 2019) and the LiteBIRD satellite at δr ≈ 0.001 (LiteBIRD
Collaboration 2023), so a LiteBIRD non-detection means r below about 0.002. Only LiteBIRD-level precision separates the two outcomes:

- **r ≈ 0.003 detected** (the Starobinsky / k = 2 value): strong evidence for plateau
  inflation, and the end of the ekpyrotic bounce and the CPT-mirror universe.
- **Nothing above ~0.002:** Starobinsky and Higgs inflation die. Inflation survives
  only in models with tiny r (like k = 4 above). The no-inflation bounce and the
  CPT-mirror universe gain ground.

Either way, the bet in section 4 gets tested within about ten to fifteen years
(LiteBIRD is planned to launch in early 2033 and observe for three years).

## 9. Testing section 5 with real Planck data

Section 5 proposed that the weak signal at the largest angles of the CMB could be a
trace of what came before inflation. The simplest physical version of that idea is a
**cutoff**. Suppose inflation lasted only just long enough. Then the very largest
scales we can see were created right at its start, when inflation was not yet running
smoothly, so they carry less power. Contaldi et al. (2003) wrote this as
P(k) = P_ΛCDM(k) × [1 − exp(−(k/k_c)^λ)], where k_c is the cutoff scale.

`lowl_cutoff.py` fits that cutoff to the Planck 2018 temperature spectrum at the
largest angles (multipoles ℓ = 2–29, the Commander component-separated spectrum). It
uses CAMB, which agrees with Planck's own best-fit theory curve to 0.4%. Everything
except the cutoff is fixed at the Planck 2018 mean values (Planck 2018 VI, Table 2). `lowl_cutoff.png` shows the result.

**Method caveat.** Planck's real low-ℓ likelihood is not reproduced. I use the
standard approximate form (a Wishart likelihood per multipole). It is calibrated so
that its error bars match the ones Planck publishes, which implies an effective sky
fraction of about 0.74 (0.39 for the quadrupole). Full-sky weighting gives the
quadrupole the most influence possible, and I use it as an upper bound.

**What the data show:**

| Quantity | Result |
|---|---|
| Quadrupole (ℓ = 2) vs ΛCDM expectation | 22% of the expected power |
| Average power, ℓ = 2–29, vs ΛCDM | 90% |
| Chance of a quadrupole this low in ΛCDM | 5% (full sky) to 21% (calibrated) |
| Chance of large-angle correlation S₁/₂ this low in ΛCDM | 8% to 14% |
| Best-fit cutoff | k_c ≈ 2×10⁻⁴ Mpc⁻¹ (ℓ ≈ 3, about the size of the observable universe) |
| Fit improvement from the cutoff (Δχ², 2 extra parameters) | −0.9 (calibrated) to −1.7 (full-sky upper bound): 0.5σ–0.8σ |
| Planck 2015's own fit of the same cutoff, full likelihood | Δχ² ≈ −3.4 (Planck 2015 XX), about 1.3σ |
| Cutoffs disfavoured (Δχ² ≳ 4, about 2σ) | k_c ≳ 5×10⁻⁴ Mpc⁻¹ for sharp cutoffs, ≳ 6×10⁻⁴ for soft ones (suppression reaching ℓ ≈ 7–8 or beyond) |

The S₁/₂ numbers are for the full-sky Commander spectrum. Computed directly on the
masked sky outside the Galaxy, the missing large-angle correlation is much rarer:
0.025% in WMAP (Copi et al. 2009) and 0.03–0.33% in WMAP and Planck maps, depending
on the map and mask (Copi et al. 2015, MNRAS 451, 2978). How rare it really is depends on that choice and on
whether choosing the statistic after seeing the data inflates its significance. That
debate is unresolved (Planck 2018 VII).

**Would polarisation settle it?** Suppose the best-fit cutoff is real. A perfect,
noise-free E-mode polarisation map over 70% of the sky (LiteBIRD-like) would add only
about Δχ² ≈ 1.6. The cutoff model predicts 10–30% less E-mode power at ℓ = 2–10, but
there are only a few independent patterns on the sky at those scales. Added to what
this analysis sees in temperature, the total would be Δχ² ≈ 2.4–3.3, still below 2σ.
(Adding the same gain to Planck's own −3.4 would give about 5, but that mixes two
different analyses.) A stronger cutoff, at the edge of what the temperature data
still allow (k_c ≈ 4–5×10⁻⁴ Mpc⁻¹), would leave a bigger mark: E-modes would add
Δχ² ≈ 2–8, which could bring the total to about 2σ, but not much further. These
forecasts are optimistic, because τ (the reionisation optical depth) was held fixed,
and in reality it partly mimics a cutoff in E-modes.

**Conclusion:**

1. **The data are consistent with "just enough inflation", but they don't ask for it.**
   The cutoff improves the fit by less than 1σ in this analysis, and by about 1.3σ in
   Planck's. What the data do say, at about 2σ, is that inflation's "start-up" phase did not
   leave its mark on scales smaller than about ℓ ≈ 7–8.
2. **The CMB alone probably can never prove a pre-inflation cutoff.** The obstacle is
   cosmic variance, not instruments. The largest scales contain only a handful of
   independent patterns. Even perfect temperature and polarisation maps would leave the
   best-fit cutoff below 2σ, and the strongest cutoffs still allowed at only about 2σ.
3. **The more promising test is a prediction made before the data exist.** Copi et al. (2013) worked
   out, in advance, how polarisation should correlate with temperature if the missing
   large-angle correlation is just a rare ΛCDM fluke. That gives a fair test of the
   "fluke" explanation, though they expect only about a 25% chance that it is decisive.
   Physical models make their own advance predictions, for example the
   loop-quantum-cosmology bounce (Ashtekar–Gupt 2016;
   Ashtekar et al. 2020). LiteBIRD's large-scale polarisation maps will test
   these predictions without the after-the-fact problem, though cosmic variance limits
   how decisive any of them can be.

**Effect on the bet in section 4: none.** The testable version of section 5 survives,
but as a "weak, possibly never decisive" signal, not as a smoking gun.

## 10. The other sharp test: neutrino masses

Section 8 tested the stories with r. The CPT-mirror universe (Boyle–Finn–Turok 2018)
makes a second sharp prediction that has nothing to do with the CMB: **the lightest
neutrino has exactly zero mass**, and the light neutrinos are Majorana particles (each
is its own antiparticle). None of the other six stories predicts anything about
neutrino masses, so this test is specific to the CPT mirror.

**What is established.** There are three neutrino masses. Oscillation experiments
measure only the *differences* of their squares, so once you choose the lightest mass,
all three are fixed. The order is still open: "normal" (two light, one heavier) or
"inverted" (one light, two heavier). Using NuFIT 6.0 (Esteban et al. 2024),
`neutrino_mass.py` computes:

| | Normal ordering | Inverted ordering |
|---|---|---|
| Masses if the lightest is exactly 0 (the CPT-mirror point) | 0, 8.7, 50.1 meV | 49.1, 49.8, 0 meV |
| Sum of the three (what cosmology measures) | 58.8 meV | 98.9 meV |
| m_β (what tritium beta decay measures) | 8.8 meV | 48.8 meV |
| m_ββ (what neutrinoless double beta decay measures, if Majorana) | 1.5–3.7 meV | 18–48 meV |

(1 meV = 0.001 eV. The m_ββ ranges come from two unknown phases.) These sums are the
smallest the two orderings allow. A nonzero lightest mass raises all of them, as
`neutrino_mass.png` shows.

**Current limits:**

| Measurement | Limit | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Sum of masses, DESI DR2 BAO + CMB, ΛCDM | < 64 meV (95%) | DESI Collaboration 2025 (DR2 Results II) |
| Sum, DESI DR2 BAO + Lyman-α full shape + CMB (Planck + ACT + SPT), ΛCDM | < 59 meV (95%); < 56.5 meV without the Lyman-α full shape | DESI Collaboration 2026 (DR2 Results IV) |
| Lightest mass, DESI DR2 + CMB combined with oscillation data | < 23 meV (95%); the cosmological sum is in 3σ tension with the oscillation minimum | Elbers et al. 2025 (DESI DR2 neutrino paper, arXiv:2503.14744) |
| Sum, CMB (Planck + ACT + SPT) + DESI DR2 + DES supernovae | < 52 meV (ΛCDM, flat prior); < 92 meV with a prior that respects oscillation data | Hou et al. 2026 |
| Sum, DESI DR2 + CMB, evolving dark energy (w0wa) | < 160 meV (95%) | DESI Collaboration 2025 |
| m_β, direct (tritium) | < 0.45 eV (90%) | KATRIN Collaboration 2025 |
| m_ββ, xenon-136 | < 28–122 meV (90%), range from nuclear-physics uncertainty | KamLAND-Zen Collaboration, PRL 135, 262501 (2025) |
| Ordering, oscillation data | normal preferred, Δχ² = 6.1 | NuFIT 6.0 (with Super-Kamiokande atmospheric data) |
| Ordering, adding the first JUNO data | normal preferred, Δχ² = 4.6 (without atmospheric data) or 9.4 (with) | Esteban et al., JHEP 04 (2026) 089 |

**What follows:**

1. **Cosmology sits at the CPT-mirror point, and maybe even below it.** The sum limits
   (< 59–64 meV) sit right at the normal-ordering minimum of 58.8 meV. They disfavour
   the inverted ordering at more than 3σ in ΛCDM, but DESI warn that this is not strong
   evidence for the normal ordering either, because most of the allowed range is
   inconsistent with *both*. Done properly, by combining with oscillation data, the
   lightest mass is below 23 meV (Elbers et al. 2025). (Reading the 64 meV limit
   naively off the curve gives about 4 meV, but that limit assumes a prior starting at
   zero total mass, so it overstates the constraint.) The data actually *prefer* a sum
   below the 58.8 meV that oscillations require. Some analyses frame this as a
   preference for a "negative" mass: 2.8–3.3σ with the first DESI data (Elbers et al.
   2024, "mirage" paper; Green–Meyers 2024), and 3σ with DR2 (Elbers et al. 2025). The
   usual explanations are excess CMB lensing or dark energy that evolves. With evolving
   dark energy the limit loosens to 160 meV, so the lightest mass could be tens of meV.
   So the CPT prediction agrees with the data, but it can't take credit: the good fit
   is partly a symptom of a tension that nobody understands yet.
2. **"Exactly zero" can never be proven, only refuted.** A lightest mass of 10 meV
   raises the sum by only 15.6 meV. Seeing that at 2σ needs a precision of about 8 meV
   on the sum. DESI DR2 + CMB quote 20 meV, but that width comes from a
   posterior cut off at zero. Without the cut, the measurement is about 50 meV wide
   (σ = 53 meV when negative values are allowed, Elbers et al. 2025), which could detect only a
   lightest mass of about 47 meV or more. A result that *would* kill the CPT mirror is
   a measured sum clearly above the floor that holds up across dark-energy models. For
   example, 80 ± 10 meV in the normal ordering means a lightest mass of about 13 meV.
3. **Neutrinoless double beta decay gives a cleaner test than I expected.**
   - *Normal ordering:* the CPT-mirror point predicts m_ββ = 1.5–3.7 meV. That is below
     the reach of the next generation of experiments (LEGEND-1000 aims at a 3σ
     discovery reach of 9–21 meV, depending on nuclear physics, per its 2021
     pre-conceptual design report; nEXO claims somewhat deeper reach). Any signal
     those experiments *do* see is well above the 3.7 meV maximum: 9 meV already needs
     a lightest mass of at least 6.8 meV, and 21 meV needs about 20 meV.
     So in the normal ordering, a detection of neutrinoless double beta decay by the
     next generation would rule out the CPT mirror. This assumes the decay goes
     through ordinary light-neutrino exchange.
   - *Inverted ordering:* the CPT mirror predicts 18–48 meV (the lower end is 15–21 meV
     across the 3σ range of the mixing angle θ₁₂). Only the optimistic next-generation
     sensitivities cover the whole range. A non-detection below about 15 meV would kill
     it, or kill the Majorana assumption, which the same paper also predicts.
4. **Beta decay can't test it.** m_β = 8.8 meV is fifty times below KATRIN's limit.

**Bottom line.** The neutrino test is slower than the r test of section 8. The decisive
inputs are the mass ordering (JUNO, within a few years), a cosmological sum with
~5–10 meV precision that holds up across dark-energy models, and next-generation
double beta decay (2030s). The CPT mirror passes every check so far. The odd
"below the minimum" preference in cosmology is the main thing to watch: if it goes
away because of evolving dark energy, the room for a nonzero lightest mass reopens.
If it goes away because a nonzero sum is measured, the CPT mirror could be dead.

**Effect on the bet in section 4: none yet.** This adds a second, independent way
the CPT-mirror story could die within about 10–15 years.
